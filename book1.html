The I Am and the Tree

Before this story begins, I want to name the question that follows it. In the Greek Gospel of John, ego eimi means “I am,” or, with emphasis, “I, I am.” In Exodus 3:14, the Hebrew ehyeh asher ehyeh is traditionally rendered “I am that I am”; because ehyeh is an imperfect form of “to be,” it can also be read “I will be what I will be.” “I am becoming” is a spiritual reflection on that unfolding sense, not a literal translation.

The Tree of Life below is presented first in Hebrew as a map for reflection. In established Kabbalistic tradition it represents ten sefirot, and some diagrams add Da’at as an eleventh point. This book uses the Tree as a personal ethical image: every idea, emotion, relationship, and claim of spiritual meaning must be tested by balance, humility, truth, and the Golden Rule.

How to Read a Sacred String

Hebrew is written and read from right to left. That direction is a feature of the language, not proof that every word has a hidden reverse message. A careful reader begins with the exact string, its ordinary grammar, and its context. Only then may the reader add a personal metaphor, and the metaphor should remain named as metaphor.

The phrase אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh asher ehyeh) in Exodus 3:14 has three parts: אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh, “I am/will be”), אֲשֶׁר (asher, often “that/who/what”), and אֶהְיֶה again. Its future or unfolding sense allows a reader to reflect on becoming, but the phrase should not be detached from its biblical setting or made to say more than the language can support.

The Hebrew verb לְדַבֵּר (ledaber) means “to speak.” Its root letters are ד־ב־ר (davar), a root associated with speaking and words; davar can also mean “thing” or “matter” depending on context. That closeness offers a grounded image for the book: words are not empty. They become matters that people must carry, answer for, and repair.

Letters, Mirrors, and Memory

Letters can carry traditional symbolic associations. Lamed is often pictured as a staff or goad and is linked in later teaching with learning; dalet is associated with a door; bet with a house; and resh with a head. These associations can form a useful meditation: guidance, opening, dwelling, and awareness. They are not a substitute for Hebrew grammar or a claim that each letter independently translates to one fixed idea.

A mirror reading is strongest when it reveals a question rather than announces a verdict. The forward string may help a reader reflect on receiving words with care. The reversed shape may help that reader reflect on sending words into the world. Reversing letters does not create a new, established Hebrew definition. It creates a visual and contemplative prompt: what enters memory, what stays in the body, what reaches the mouth, and what consequence follows?

Memory belongs in this work because memories give symbols their human weight. A remembered room, a parent’s voice, a childhood prayer, a sentence that wounded, or a sentence that helped can make an abstract word immediate. But memory is personal testimony, not independent proof of a historical, scientific, or theological claim. Hold it with respect, compare it with what can be checked, and let its best use be compassion for another person’s unseen story.

The seven dimensions named in this book are not a scientific or doctrinal claim. They are seven ways of examining a choice: body, emotion, thought, relationship, conscience, spirit, and action. The physical and spiritual are two dimensions of the same work. A tesseract is a mathematical figure, not a cosmic black hole; here it is a metaphor for the fact that an emotion can have more than one visible face and still shape a whole life.

The film Interstellar offers a familiar imaginative picture: its tesseract scene represents time as something that can be seen through many connected moments. The film is fiction, but the mathematical word is real. A tesseract is a four-dimensional hypercube: where a square has 4 corners and a cube has 8, a tesseract has 16 vertices, 32 edges, 24 square faces, and 8 cube-shaped cells. We can only draw or model its three-dimensional projections, never hold its full four-dimensional form in the hand.

A Rubik’s Cube is a useful physical comparison because one turn changes several visible relationships at once. It is still a three-dimensional puzzle, not a tesseract, and its standard 3-by-3-by-3 form has more than 43 quintillion possible arrangements. In this book, the cube becomes a reminder to pause before making a turn in anger, fear, or desire: every choice moves more than one part of a life. The goal is not to solve a cosmic secret, but to bring the next choice back into balance through truth, compassion, and responsibility.

Cosmos, Memory, and Folded Perspectives

The Big Bang model describes an early hot, dense state of the observable universe followed by expansion. A “Big Crunch” is a possible name for a future recollapse in some cosmological models, but it is not the current scientific conclusion; observations indicate that cosmic expansion is accelerating. Birth and death can therefore be used here as human metaphors for beginning, change, release, and return, not as evidence that the universe or a person’s life literally repeats in one prescribed cycle.

Computer science gives a more concrete language for one part of this image. A memory address is a location used by a program to find stored data. A pointer or reference connects one piece of information to another without making them identical. Data structures such as trees, graphs, and linked lists model relationships by following these connections one step at a time. Patterns and alignments appear across many fields because minds and computers both compare structure, sequence, repetition, and relation; a resemblance can be worth investigating, but it is not proof that two things share one hidden cause.

In the book’s metaphor, each experience is a folded perspective: one moment can be connected to memories, emotions, choices, and relationships that are not visible from a single angle. Calling all things “folded tesseracts” is poetic language for this layered connectedness, not a statement of physics. The practical question remains grounded: what connection can be checked, what is only an interpretation, and what action would increase care for the people affected?

Synchronicity can name the felt experience of meaningful coincidence. It may invite reflection, journaling, or conversation, but it should not replace evidence or require anyone to take unsafe action. The flash of life sometimes reported near death, likewise, is a serious human experience that deserves compassion and clinical care, not a proof of a cosmic mechanism.

The pattern in the final source note can be checked directly. Doubling gives $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and so on; reducing each result to a single decimal digit by repeatedly adding its digits produces a repeating sequence. This is called a digital root pattern, and it depends on base 10. It can be visually compelling and useful as a prompt to study number systems, but it is not evidence of a hidden energy system, a code to fate, or a universal hierarchy.

The next book develops the same map through five symbolic elements. Earth names grounding when fear scatters attention; water names feeling and the work of grief; wind names thought, language, and changing perspective; fire names courage, anger, desire, and action; and aether names the quiet center of conscience that holds the others in balance. These are metaphors for inner life, not literal claims about altered bodies, hidden substances, or entities.

Children, Growing Up, and Learning to Speak

A child learns language before understanding its full weight. At first there is a cry, then a name, then the discovery that a word can ask for comfort, offer love, wound another person, or begin repair. Growing up includes learning that speech is not only sound. It is a responsibility. Children watch the words adults choose long before they can explain what those words mean, and they learn whether speech is used to guide, to listen, to control, or to care.

The Hebrew infinitive ledaber, “to speak,” is written לדבר and read from right to left. In this book’s symbolic reflection, its letters can be held as a movement from guidance to thought: lamed, the staff or learning; dalet, the door; bet, the house; and resh, the head. The image is not a literal translation of the individual letters. It is a meditation on how a person opens attention, receives guidance, holds it within the body, and brings it into a conscious mind.

When the letters are reversed as רבדל, they do not form another established Hebrew word or a second grammatical meaning. Yet the reversed image can still serve as a mirror for self-examination: an idea begins in the mind, fills the house of the body, reaches the door of the mouth, and enters the world through speech. A spoken word may become a staff that steadies someone, or a weapon that harms them. The choice belongs to the speaker.

For a child and for an adult, the Golden Rule gives language its direction: speak to another person in the way you would need to be spoken to when you are afraid, learning, wrong, or trying to begin again. This is how speech becomes less an expression of ego and more a practice of truth, mercy, and responsibility.

The Language We Inherit

Children do not first learn language from a rulebook. They learn it from faces, tone, repetition, correction, stories, and the way adults speak when they believe no one is listening. Later, school gives them grammar: the shared patterns that help a sentence carry meaning from one mind to another. Grammar matters because clarity matters. A well-placed word can prevent confusion, a question can invite understanding, and a truthful sentence can begin repair.

But rules are tools, not idols. A person may use perfect grammar to avoid saying anything real. We see it in public statements that apologize without naming harm, in corporate language that calls people “resources,” and in arguments online where winning matters more than understanding. This is the inefficient noise of our age: many words, little listening; endless information, little wisdom; language polished until no one can tell who is responsible.

The answer is not to abandon learning or to reject careful speech. It is to remember why we speak. Before repeating a slogan, posting an accusation, or hiding behind a complicated phrase, ask: Is this true? Is it necessary? Is it clear? Does it honor the person who must receive it? These are grammar rules for the heart. They ask us to make language serve connection rather than ego.

Growing up means learning that every voice has consequences. A child needs patience while learning the rules. An adult needs humility when the rules become a mask. The strongest sentence is not always the cleverest one. Often it is the plain sentence that tells the truth, accepts responsibility, and leaves room for another person to answer.

Part 2: The History

History, when it is honest, is not simply a record of wars, kings, and dates. It is the memory of the human heart learning, forgetting, and returning again to the same questions. We ask where we came from, who is guiding our steps, and why so many men and women have tried to own the truth while claiming to serve the truth.

The history of God is not written only in temples and scripture. It is written in the quiet places where the soul is forced to decide whether it will kneel before ego or submit to remembrance. Every age has its own idol, and every age has its own voice trying to tell us that the way is narrow, the way is hidden, and the way is only for a chosen few.

When a person begins to search sincerely, the old stories change shape. The names of Allah become more than sounds; they become doors. The name Ana is not a boast; it is not vanity dressed in language. It is a reminder that the divine is not removed from the human. It is present in the self that chooses truth over fear, forgiveness over pride, and remembrance over illusion.

This is where the history of the world becomes personal. We are not only watching events happen around us; we are living inside the tension between the voice of the ego and the voice of the heart. One tells us to be admired, rewarded, and defended. The other tells us to be honest, to be humble, and to remember that the soul is not a possession but a trust.

The false history is easy to sell. It creates kings, armies, priests, and systems of fear. It teaches obedience before understanding. It tells people that they must be afraid to search, because searching may reveal that the walls they were taught to worship were built by men and not by God. But the true history is older than all of that. It is the history of returning.

One of the great deceptions of the modern world is the temptation to confuse the name of God with the image of power. We speak of God in many voices, but we do not always hear the stillness behind the noise. The human race has created monuments, doctrines, and institutions in the name of holiness, but too often these structures become a replacement for the sincerity that once guided them.

This is why the story must be told carefully. We cannot treat the spiritual path like a public performance. We cannot demand revelation from God while refusing to examine the motives of our own hearts. The path is not a prize to display; it is a burden of truth, a discipline of the soul, and a surrender to a wisdom that cannot be reduced to politics, religion, or ego.

Ana is linked to Grace, to mercy, to the personal recognition of the divine within the self. It is not a lesson in self-worship. It is a lesson in remembering that God is not distant from the one who seeks Him with sincerity. The problem is not that God is hidden. The problem is that the ego has become so loud that people cannot hear the silence in which the truth is always speaking.

So we begin this second part of the series by asking a question that history has never truly answered in a final way: what happens when a person stops worshiping the image and starts seeking the reality? What happens when fear is replaced by remembrance, and remembrance is tested by obedience? This is not the history of a single man. It is the history of every soul that must decide whether it will carry the light or hide in the shadow of its own pride.

If the first book showed the cost of sin and the shock of encounter, this second part will show the deeper truth behind the encounter: that history is not only what happened, but what we were meant to learn from it. The lesson is not simple. It is not comfortable. But it is necessary.

And so we continue, not with the arrogance of certainty, but with the humility of honest seekers. We ask God to keep us from mistaking our own voice for His. We ask Him to guide us through confusion, and to help us remember that no religion, no leader, and no institution can stand where the heart has not already surrendered.

That is the beginning of the history we are trying to understand. Not the history of conquest, but the history of remembrance. Not the history of control, but the history of release. Not the history of men declaring themselves righteous, but the history of souls learning to walk in the light of the truth that is already written inside them.

Peace be upon the sincere. May we be guided toward the truth, and may we be given the courage to carry it without corruption.

The history of the soul is the battle between grace and ego, between the remembrance of God and the illusion of control.

The Tree of Balance

At the beginning of the Tree is Crown; at its earthly end is Sovereignty. Between them are wisdom and understanding, strength and lovingkindness, beauty, victory and empathy, and foundation. Read as a spiritual equation, the Tree asks whether opposing forces can meet without one consuming the other. Strength without lovingkindness becomes cruelty. Lovingkindness without strength becomes helplessness. Wisdom without understanding becomes pride. Foundation without honest action cannot hold.

This is the Golden Rule of balance. Whether the subject is a person, an emotion, an idea, or an image from a sacred story, it must be tested by its fruit. Does it make us more truthful? Does it protect the dignity of another person? Does it make room for correction and care? If it fails those tests, it cannot carry us toward holiness or sovereignty.

Seven Desires, Seven Practices

The seven deadly sins can be read as desires that have lost their balance: pride wants to stand above others; greed wants to keep what should be shared; lust turns another person into an object; envy resents another’s good; gluttony cannot accept enough; wrath makes anger its master; and sloth refuses the work love requires. The traditional seven virtues offer a counter-practice: humility for pride, charity for greed, chastity for lust, kindness for envy, temperance for gluttony, patience for wrath, and diligence for sloth.

The purpose is not to condemn a person for having an emotion or desire. Desire becomes dangerous when it rules the whole self and makes us forget the Golden Rule. When anger rises, practice patience before action. When envy rises, practice kindness and gratitude. When pride rises, practice humility and listen. The good desire is the one that can remain truthful, merciful, and responsible toward another person.

Right, Left, and the Center

On the Tree, the right side is commonly associated with expansion, generosity, and lovingkindness; the left side with restraint, discernment, and strength; the middle pillar with balance and integration. The two sides can be read as mirrors, not enemies. Mercy needs a boundary, and judgment needs compassion. Beauty at the center is the work of bringing both into a life that neither collapses into indulgence nor hardens into control.

Hebrew letters and their traditional numerical values invite careful study through gematria and other interpretive practices. A reader may explore these patterns as a way of asking deeper questions about language and symbol. Numbers, correspondences, and mirrors do not decide what is true on their own. The lasting test is still the same: does the interpretation lead toward honesty, compassion, humility, and good action?

The Hilt: Mercy and Integration

A sword is an image of division: a line between choices, a boundary, an argument, or a moment of judgment. In this book, the two edges stand for opposing movements that appear across a human life: self and other, assertion and restraint, anger and fear, holding on and letting go. The image is not a call to conflict. It is a way to see that an unheld division can keep cutting long after the original question has passed.

The hilt is where a blade can be held without harming the hand. As a metaphor, it is the practice that keeps strong feeling from becoming harm: pause, name the feeling, listen for the other person’s dignity, and choose a response that can be answered for. Mercy is not passivity or the denial of boundaries. It is the center that prevents strength from becoming cruelty and prevents compassion from becoming the abandonment of truth.

Some symbolic systems use 3, 6, and 9 to represent polarity, relation, and integration. In ordinary arithmetic, $3 + 3 = 6$, $6 + 6 = 12$, and $3 + 6 = 9$. In digit-sum numerology, 12 may be reduced to 3. Those are different operations and should not be confused. This book uses the 3-6-9 pattern only as a reflective prompt: two distinct positions may be brought into a third practice of understanding, mercy, and accountable action.

The sheath completes the image. A tool of division should not remain drawn when the work of discernment is over. To sheath it is to end the cycle of retaliation, keep a boundary without continuing the fight, and return the mind and body to safety. The whole map is held not by a number, a diagram, or a symbol, but by what its reader does next.

The maze of the inner life is not escaped through fear or secret knowledge. We descend by presence, discernment, and action: noticing what is real, separating an impulse from a command, and choosing the next compassionate step. The Tree does not ask us to leave the world. It asks us to become responsible within it.
